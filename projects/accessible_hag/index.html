Towards More Accessible Video Games: Part 1 - Overthinking Everything

Sep 16, 2022

I mentioned recently a desire to create more accessible computer games. In this blog, I briefly cover some early findings towards that goal.

Introduction

Long ago in a distant land, some friends and I started writing a procedurally generated dungeon crawler named hag. Much more recently, I had the idea to try to make it more accessible to those with visual impairments. This seems relatively straightforward, as hag has a pretty simple internal representation of the world. There is a list of enemies, a list of items on the ground, a list of rooms, and a list of hallways connecting them. Right now, things close enough to the player are drawn on the screen using terminal graphics. Unfortunately, I don’t think that plays nicely with screen readers or other assistive technologies.

There are three main points of interest - the main game window, which is full of pound signs, dots, slashes, and other characters, the status window, which describes the current status of the player as well as presents a key for the main game window, and the flavortext window, which just provides a log of flavortext for the player to read.

The status window and flavortext window are probably OK for screenreaders, though the key for the game window is probably not helpful. The main game window, however, seems entirely useless for assistive technologies. I suppose it could be used to play the game, but that experience seems miserable, and we can definitely do better.

So, what if instead of rendering out nearby content (the player @ sign, the k for kobold, / for strength weapon, etc.), we generated a list of nearby features? The main game window above could instead be rendered as the following:

You are in room three.
There is a kobold two paces west.
There is a Strength Weapon one pace east and four paces south.
There is a potion five paces east and three paces south.
There is a vertical hallway six paces west and four paces north.
There is a North-East hallway six paces west and two paces north.

This gives enough information to render the game playable, but perhaps it would not be enjoyable. The issue is it is very repetitive, which defeats the purpose of a rogue-like.

High Tech Space Wizardry

Obviously the solution to this problem is to take that output and throw it at a language model. I took that output and pre-pended “Rewrite the following more succinctly, with more flourish: " and let text-davinci-002 loose on it. Here is what it produced:

You are in room three. A kobold lurks two paces west, while a Strength Weapon can be found one pace east and four paces south. A potion rests five paces east and three paces south, and a vertical hallway yawns six paces west and four paces north. A North-East hallway also lies six paces west and two paces north.

Woah! That has a lot more flavor than the laundry-list we produced programatically! Let’s try again!

You are in room three. To your west is a kobold, and to your east and south is a Strength Weapon. Five paces east and three paces south is a potion, and six paces west and four paces north is a vertical hallway. Finally, six paces west and two paces north is a North-East hallway.

Hmm, it like, just ignored half the details. Maybe that was a fluke.

There is some good variety, but that comes at the cost of accuracy and consistency. Perhaps some other model could check that the output from GPT-3 was accurate to the prompt, but that’s introducing a lot of complexity to a game compiled with C89 and curses graphics.

Back to Basics

This problem seems like one well suited to more traditional procedural generation techniques. Rather than have an AI model write our flavortexts, we could write a bunch of paragraphs describing situations and then slot in the necessary information afterwards. One could create a simplistic English grammar and expand it out until it has enough objects listed, and then fill in adjectives and adverbs as necessary. Either of these approaches would solve the accuracy problem, and should resolve the repetitive problem as well so long as sufficiently many options are provided. In an inevitable part 2 I will explore these options in more depth.
